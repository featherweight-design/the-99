R2
Undo a removal · one control, one surface

The moment already ships. R2 only owns the draft.

R1's struck row was a reinvention of a shipped treatment, and the audit caught it on a surface this sandbox could not see. UndoRow and deck-card-undo-tile.tsx have answered the moment-of-removal question since #185 — in list and card view, from the same mis-click window R1's opening paragraph described. R1 drew it again with a strikethrough instead of a dashed spent slot, in one view, and called it new.

So question 1 resolves the cheap way: #185 stands untouched and Going out ships alone. Nothing is retired, no shipped treatment is replaced, and the two views cannot disagree because R2 does not touch either of them. What is left is one control in one action slot that was already reserved and empty.

And R1's optimism was wrong for a better reason than the one it argued against. R1 defended it against rule 4, nothing unmounts mid-write. #185's reason is sharper and it is fatal: an optimistic re-add unmounts the receipt mid-flight, leaving a refused undo nowhere to be said. R1's whole refusal design is a tag on the row — so it needs a row that survives. The shipped code and this handoff want the same thing.

Diff against R1. README.md carries the three answers and the decisions log, STATES-restore.md the matrix, copy strings the production copy, READINESS.md what is left to rule on.

The audit's three questions, answered before the frames
1 · Replace #185, or ship Going out alone?
Going out ships alone. #185 stands. Replacing a shipped treatment in two views to gain a strikethrough over a dashed slot is a cost with no benefit attached — R1 priced the addition and never priced the retirement. And the two are not the same feature: #185 is a receipt for the tap you just made, one at a time, gone when you leave the builder. Going out is the standing record of every cut in the draft. They answer different questions and the boundary below is clean.
2 · Optimism against #185's reason
#185 wins, and R2 drops optimism. The argument is not about certainty, it is about where the refusal goes. R1 designed the refusal as a tag and a changed control on the row; optimism destroys that row before the server answers. R1 then invented a revert to put it back — machinery whose only job was to undo the optimism. The row simply stays, and the refusal lands where it was always designed to land. The revert is deleted with it.
3 · Does the card-view tile change?
No — and that is the answer, not a gap. The row-only worry was real for R1, which would have left list view with a strikethrough and card view with a spent tile. R2 changes neither view: both keep #185 exactly as shipped, so they agree by construction. Going out lives on /decks/[id]/changes, which has no card view to disagree with.

The seam is the word “every”. #185 cannot answer “I pasted a list and want three of the seventeen cuts back” — it holds one removal, it skips quantity moves, and it is gone after a reload. Going out cannot answer “I just mis-tapped” as well as a receipt in the row you were looking at. Neither is a subset of the other, which is why shipping both costs less than reconciling them.
Two labels, and now for a better reason than R1 had. R1 argued Undo against Put back from the two clocks. 3 · The four states on one row

R1 had five — the revert is gone with the optimism

The row does not move until the server has answered — which is #185's rule, and rule 4's, and the thing R1's refusal design needed all along. A refusal is told apart from a success by three signals, none of them size: the label changes, a tag appears, and the dot goes brass. R1 claimed the action slot never changes size; it does, because each label is sized to itself — and what the two refusal states do share is one 32px slot, which is what puts their prices in a column.

Revised from R1, and the audit's framing is the reason. R1 was optimistic and argued it against rule 4. #185's argument is the one that lands: an optimistic re-add unmounts the receipt mid-flight and leaves a refused undo nowhere to be said. R1's refusal is a tag and a changed control on this row — so the row has to still be there. Optimism and the refusal design were never compatible; R1 shipped both and papered over it with a revert.
It is not a spinner and not a re-labelled button. The label does not change, nothing animates, no width is reserved for a longer string, and no other row is affected. disabled={isPending} is the pattern the remove control already ships with — one attribute, borrowed, against R1's two labels, a width-matching rule and a revert path.
So the objection to R1's spinner is honoured, not overruled. The complaint was ceremony — watching plumbing to undo a mis-tap. A dimmed label for one round trip is not ceremony; it is the smallest possible honest signal, and it is what buys a refusal that has somewhere to go.

The row does not move, and no count changes. The card is still going out. It converts in place into the shape an add with no free copy already has — holding deck, tag, price, and the action that applies in the slot the restore occupied. Because the row never left, there is nothing to revert, which is the whole simplification R2 gains.
The two differ in when they are known, which is why they are two states. claimed is discovered on submit — pre-flighting it would mean polling every cut card's claims for the life of the draft. commander is known before the tap, because DeckClaim.commander is already in the payload for every claim — so that row never offers a restore and needs no new field, per the audit.
A refusal re-prices rather than dead-ends. Decline the Pull and the card still comes back on commit, carrying its BuyTag — see frame 4. The three outcomes are pull, buy, or leave it out, and the row shows all three. That is why the refusal earns a tag and not a red sentence: a sentence frames a priced choice as a failure.

Notes for engineering
The restore can be addCardToDeck, the same call #185's undo makes. R1 specified restoreRemovedCard to preserve seniority; the audit retires that — versions.ts:175 inherits since at commit by oracle id, so any restore inside a draft preserves it. A thin wrapper is justified only if addCardToDeck's existing guard vocabulary cannot name the holding deck — that is the one thing the refusal design needs and the only reason to add anything.
Refusal reasons are a server enum carrying the deck — claimed_by_deck and is_commander_of — not strings, and with no generic no-name fallback: the value of the refusal is which deck to go and ask. Revalidate through revalidateClaims(deckId, userId), not deckSurfaces().
Not optimistic, and the reason is #185's. The row must outlive the round trip because the refusal is drawn on it. The in-flight state is the control disabling — no spinner, no label change, no reserved width.
No new storage. Going out is draftDiff.removes, which already enumerates every cut. R2 adds no client state at all — R1's per-visit struck-row state is gone with the struck row.
The commander flag needs nothing new. DeckClaim.commander is in the payload for every claim, so the row is drawn refused from the start. READINESS 20 is closed.
One shipped string is edited: review.outNote, whose first clause is now false. pullCta is a second if the demotion is taken. The new strings are a restore block in the same file.
Flagged, not edited: review.lede — “nothing here is a decision except the pulls” is now wrong by one. A ratification question, not a Design call.
One live-region message per settle, on the existing region: Necrotic Ooze is back in the deck. 101 cards, 16 going out. On a refusal, the named reason once. No motion anywhere.
Verify the commit sheet's money block counts every BuyTag row rather than a list scoped to draftDiff.adds. If it is the latter, a restored-but-unpurchased card is invisible to it and the buy total understates by exactly the frame-4 case. This is the one claim to check before estimating rather than after.
Card-row parity, and what the audit closed
Gap 1 is closed, in R2's favour and then made moot. The audit confirms the remove control is a trailing button with a 44px phone target and the row carries a BuyTag, so R1's −12px argument held. R2 no longer needs it — it adds nothing to a decklist row.
Name + one availability slot: held. The refused row is the pullable add row's own ratified marker-plus-price pair, reached from the other column.
Every addition names what it displaced: the Going out row's action slot was reserved and empty since R1.2. Nothing is displaced.
No row grows, and none is two-line — at any width, refusals included. Canonical metrics unchanged: 34px links, 44px targets, radius 7, gap 9.
Words, never colour alone: the tag word, the control's label and the row's accessible name all carry it. No hue is load-bearing.
Unchanged and not re-opened, per the audit: the two clocks, strikethrough-over-left-rule (now academic), the two labels, no bulk restore, no motion, no expiry warning, the refusal enum, revalidateClaims.
