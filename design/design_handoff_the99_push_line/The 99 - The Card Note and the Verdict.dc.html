The card note and the verdict

Round 4 of 5 · §4.1 premise withdrawn 2026-09-21

Hugh’s ruling splits one object into two. The card note is a claim about a card — cheap, immediate, and true whether or not the deck has been analysed. The verdict is a claim about a deck at a moment, and it now exists only because someone asked for it or committed a version. The two were one sentence when a verdict was free. It is not free any more, and the round is two features.

the five calls
1 · The note’s bracket clause reads against the declared bracket, not a computed one. A declaration is the player’s own statement of what they are building; it does not go stale when they add a card, and it is what your bracket most naturally means. With no declaration the note makes no deck claim at all and prints the card fact alone. 1b 2 · Two registers, because the detections differ. Game Changer is a list membership and is stated flatly. Mass land denial and extra turns are read off oracle text, so they are attributed to us — we read this as — and the bracket clause becomes conditional on that reading. Neither register ever names the deck’s standing. 1b 3 · The analyze trigger is the verdict block’s own control, and the tab carries the only signal. A dot on the Analysis tab label when undetermined edits exist — no banner, no toast, no button on the decklist. The app may say the answer is old; it may not nag for permission to refresh it. 1d 4 · Asked and committed read identically, and behave differently. Same sentence, same marks — the verdict is not two verdicts. The provenance line differs, and only the committed one is immune to staleness, because it describes a snapshot that cannot change. 1e 5 · The stale verdict follows Round 0’s form and loses its counterweight. Demoted, tagged, dated with its own denominator — but Round 0 could write recounted just now beside a stale sentence, and nothing recounts any more. The control that ends the staleness takes that slot, and the card notes are what stay true in the meantime. 1f 6 · §4.5, re-read: the note does not persist, and does not need to. The card is in the deck now and Round 1’s marks carry its facts on the decklist row. Nothing about the add is lost, because nothing about the add was ever a verdict. 1g
1a One sentence, split in two — and what each half can still say §4.1 withdrawn

The push line worked because a verdict was a free by-product of every keystroke. Once standing is determined only on request, a per-add sentence about the deck is a sentence the app is not entitled to write — it would either recompute the thing Hugh ruled we do not recompute, or quote a reading from four edits ago as though it were now. What survives the ruling is everything that was true of the card, and that turns out to be most of what the sentence was carrying.

the R2 push line, and the clause that dies
If you add this That would be your 4th game changer, and Upgraded allows 3. It moves what your cards permit from Upgraded to Optimized or cEDH.
The first clause is a card fact and a rules fact. This card is a Game Changer and Upgraded allows three of them are both true without analysing anything; only the ordinal needs the deck, and a count of Game Changers in a list is not a verdict. The struck clause is the whole of what the ruling removes — a range-to-range statement about the deck’s standing, which now requires an act.
what each object keeps
the card note What the card is. What the rules say about that kind of card. Whether the declared bracket permits it. The count of its kind already in the list. On both add surfaces, always, with no analysis run. the verdict What the deck’s cards permit, the receipts behind it, and the upgrade path out. On the Analysis tab, on ask or on commit, and nowhere else. The split is a gain, not a consolation. The old sentence could only speak when bracketDelta found a transition, which is rare; the note speaks whenever the card carries a fact, which is far more often and is the part a player adding cards can actually act on. And it survives the one thing that killed the push line: it is still true four edits later.
1b Likely, never determined — and the two registers that makes constraint

Hedging everything equally is the failure mode here. Of the three detections, one is a list lookup and two are readings of oracle text — and a note that says we think this might be a Game Changer about a card on WotC’s published list teaches the player that our hedges are decoration, at which point the hedge on the two that need it stops working. So the uncertainty is spent where it is real, and stated as authorship rather than as doubt: we read this as, which is checkable, rather than possibly, which is not.

register 1 — a list fact, stated flatly
About this card
A game changer, and your 4th. Upgraded allows 3.
No hedge, because there is nothing to hedge. Membership of WotC’s list is a lookup, the count is arithmetic over the list the player is looking at, and the allowance is quoted rules text. Three determinate facts and no claim about the deck’s standing — note that it says Upgraded allows 3, never you are at Upgraded.
register 2 — a reading, attributed
About this card
We read this as mass land denial, which no bracket with limits allows. If that’s wrong for how you play it, the reading is ours, not the rules’.
The hedge is on the detection and nowhere else. No bracket with limits allows it stays flat, because that clause is rules text and is not in doubt — the uncertainty is entirely about whether this card is that kind of card. The last clause is the honest part: it tells the player which half to argue with, and it is the only place the app admits a reading can be wrong without pretending the rules might be.
rejected registers
“This may push your bracket.” The exact claim the ruling removed, in softer clothes. May does not make a deck statement into a card statement. A confidence percentage. The detections are boolean and their error rate is unmeasured; a figure would be invented precision on top of a partial reading. “Possibly mass land denial.” Doubt with no author and no remedy. We read this as costs the same and tells the player whose claim it is. A warning colour. --warning or --danger on a note that is likely by construction would be the surface’s loudest signal attached to its least certain claim. The note is --fact ink and body text, always.
the bracket clause — which bracket, when the deck’s standing is unknown
Declared, where there is a declaration. Round 3 already has the player stating what they are building. A declaration cannot go stale from an edit — it is an intention, not a measurement — so the note can cite it on every add without recomputing anything, and your bracket means the bracket the player said they were playing. It is also the only reading under which the note is advice rather than trivia. Undeclared: no deck clause at all. The note prints the card fact and the rules fact and stops. It does not fall back to the last determined verdict, which is the trap: that reading may be four edits old, and a note that silently quotes a stale verdict is worse than the per-add verdict the ruling just removed, because nothing on the row says how old it is. Declared but not yet determined is fine. The note is about a rule and a declaration, and neither needs an analysis to have run. This is the case that makes the split worth having: a player who has declared Upgraded and never once opened the Analysis tab still gets told, on the row, that the card they are about to add is their fourth Game Changer.
One consequence worth stating for eng: the note needs the declaration and the card facts, and it does not need buildDeckProfile or bracketDelta at all. Its only deck-derived figure is a count of a kind in the current list, which the add panel can compute from data it already holds. That is what makes it cheap enough to draw on every row of a 480-card pool.

1d The analyze trigger — and the one signal that leaves the tab deliverable 2

Nothing today says what’s the state of my changes?, and the temptation is to answer it everywhere — a button on the decklist, a banner after an edit, a prompt on commit. Every one of those re-imports the thing the ruling removed: a per-edit reminder that the deck has an unexamined standing is a per-edit verdict with the number left out. So the control lives where the answer lives, and exactly one mark travels.

One control, three weights, one place. Filled accent when there is no answer yet, because that is the only state where pressing it is the whole point of the tab; outlined and secondary once an answer exists, because the answer is now the subject and the button is maintenance. It never moves position between states — a player who has pressed it once knows where it is.
the dot, and what it is allowed to mean It means: there is something here you have not seen. Present when edits exist since the last determination, and when no determination exists at all. It carries no number — a count of unexamined edits invites the reader to decide whether four is enough to bother with, which is a judgement the app cannot help them make. It is not a warning. --accent, 6px, the same dot the app already uses for unseen content. Unexamined is not the same as wrong, and most edits move nothing. It clears on determination, not on visit. Opening the tab and reading a stale verdict does not make it current, so the dot stays until the answer does.
rejected triggers A button on the decklist. Puts the deck’s standing back in front of a player who is editing — the ruling with an extra click in it. A prompt after N edits. The app picking the moment the player is supposed to care. Hugh’s ruling is precisely that the player picks it. Auto-analyse on opening the tab. Defensible, and it makes the tab a page that computes when looked at; the stale state then never exists, and neither does the player’s ability to compare a verdict to the edits made since. Worth revisiting if the run is genuinely free — recorded in READINESS, not assumed here. A toast on commit. Commit already determines (1e); announcing it twice makes a checkpoint feel like an alarm.
1e Asked and committed — one verdict, two provenances deliverable 3

The verdict reads the same, and it has to. Two phrasings for one computation would tell the player the app knows something different about a committed deck than about the same 99 cards a second earlier, which is false — the function is the same function. What legitimately differs is what the answer is about: an asked verdict describes a live list that the next keystroke changes; a committed one describes a version, and a version cannot change. That difference belongs in the provenance line and in the staleness behaviour, and nowhere else.

asked — about the list as it stands
Your cards permit Upgraded determined against all 99 · 2 minutes ago
Dated, and counted. The denominator is Round 0’s and does the same work here: it tells the reader what the answer was computed over, which is the only way a later staleness tag can mean anything. This one can go stale, and will, on the next edit.
committed — about a version, permanently
Your cards permit Upgraded determined for v4 · all 99 · 21 Sept
Named, not dated-relative. 2 minutes ago is right for something about to be superseded; a version’s verdict is a record, so it takes the version’s own name and an absolute date. It never goes stale — edits after a commit belong to the draft, and the draft’s verdict is a separate, askable thing. That is also why commit is a natural determination point: the app is already snapshotting the list, and a verdict is the cheapest thing it can attach.
1f The stale verdict — Round 0’s rule holds, and its counterweight is gone deliverable 4

Yes, the verdict follows — the form transfers exactly, and the reasoning does not. Round 0 kept a stale sentence because hiding it cost the player a true description of 92 of their 99 cards to avoid being wrong about seven, and it could afford to because the verdict beside it was explicitly marked current. Under this ruling nothing recounts, so that sentence cannot be written any more — the block that was the anchor is now the thing adrift. The same argument still lands, because a bracket reading computed over 95 of 99 cards is overwhelmingly likely to be the right one; but it has to be carried by the tag and the denominator alone.

Three changes from Round 0’s stale synopsis, all forced by the ruling. The verb goes past tense — permitted, not permits — which is the cheapest honest signal available and works before the tag is read. The mark dims to 0.55 with the sentence, because here the figure is the stale thing; Round 0 could keep its verdict bright and demote only the prose. And the control takes the slot where recounted just now used to sit — it goes back to filled accent, because in this state pressing it is again the point of the tab.
what stays true while the verdict is stale The card notes, all of them. They were never derived from the verdict, so a stale verdict does not make a single note wrong — the player adding cards on the other tab is still being told what each card is and what the rules say about it. That is the split paying for itself: under the old design, a deck between analyses had no true statements on any surface. The mix strip and the counts. Counted from the list, unaffected, and explicitly not tagged — Round 0’s rule that a stale sentence never takes the figures down with it survives for everything that is still counted rather than determined.
rejected Hiding the stale verdict. Round 0’s argument, unchanged: it costs the player a reading computed over 95 of 99 cards to avoid being wrong about four, and they can see the four. A guess at whether the four mattered. Tempting — two of them are Game Changers — and it is a verdict computed on the sly to decide whether to admit the verdict is old. Greying the whole block. The upgrade path and receipts below are as old as the verdict and are demoted with it, but the block keeps its border and its contrast floor. Stale is not disabled.
