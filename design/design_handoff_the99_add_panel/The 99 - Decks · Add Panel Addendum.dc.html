Add panel addendum · #183 #177 #179 #185 #184 #176 · R3

One dock, one bar, and content pays rent.

Four of these six are the same defect twice and the same row twice. #183 and #177 are both content sitting under something pinned to the bottom of a phone, so they get one rule rather than two paddings. #179 and #185 are the two ends of removing a card — no way to do it on a phone, no way to take it back anywhere — so they get one affordance and one undo that lives where the row was. #184 deletes five sub-44px links by making the cell they duplicate the control. #176 is a product call first and a tile second; both are drawn.

Addendum to R2 of #158. The shipped Add-panel canvas and add-panel · copy strings.ts are locked and unedited; the nine copy calls inside that lock are drawn in the last section and keyed in add-panel addendum · copy strings.ts. Every state leads with the phone.

1 · The bottom rule
Three layers, one dock. Content reserves the stack’s height and never centres through it. Closes #183 and #177.
2 · Mobile remove
The same ✕, at a 44px hit box. Not swipe, not an edit mode — the row already has the control, it was hidden.
3 · Undo
The removed row stays as a spent slot carrying Undo. No toast, no timer, no second dock.
4 · The cell is the link
Five bare links go. A short cell is the control; rationale becomes one strip-level disclosure.
5 · Unowned tiles
Kept, marked buy, and shelved last. The provenance line stops claiming they are owned.
6 · Copy calls
Four em dashes, one doubled numeral, two figures that never said what they were measured against.
The rule · what owns the bottom of a phone viewport — #183 + #177
Three layers. Only one of them is negotiable.
Layer 3 · contentscrolls
Reserves the height of everything below it and centres inside that reserve, never through it. A centred empty state measured against the viewport is exactly #183.
Layer 2 · route docksticky · bottom 80px
One per route. At most. The builder’s is the quick-add bar. /decks/new’s is Create deck →. A route’s primary action does not sit near the dock — it is the dock, or it is in flow and takes its chances with the scroll.
Layer 1 · shell tab barfixed · 80px
Owns the true bottom edge on every route, at phone width only. Nothing is drawn beneath it and nothing scrolls out from under it.
The reserve
phone   padding-bottom: dock + 80px + 12px
md:      padding-bottom: dock + 12px
centred  min-height: 100% − reserve
No new token. 80px is the shipped tab-bar height already hard-coded at builder-shell.tsx as bottom-[80px]; the dock’s own height is measured, not guessed, because it is one element in one place per route.
Why a rule and not two paddings
Both tickets were filed as layout bugs on unrelated routes, and both were shipped unfixed because the fix looked like a judgement call. It is the same judgement call: a sticky element pins to the scrollport, and a page that does not know its height loses whatever it centred.
The builder already learned half of this the hard way — the quick-add bar sat under the tab bar until it was given bottom-[80px], and the comment explaining why is in the shipped source. The other half, that content owes the same arithmetic, was never written down. This is that, written down.
The rule also forbids the tempting fix. Hiding the quick-add bar on the empty builder would clear #183 in one line and delete one of the two entrances the empty state’s own copy points at. The dock is not conditional; the reserve is.
And it pre-answers the next one. Every surface that later pins a CTA low — import, export, the picker — has a rule to fail against instead of a new ticket.

The call: promote, don’t pad
Padding the page would make the button reachable at the end of a scroll through every commander in the catalogue. The button is the route’s one job; the rule says a route’s primary action is either the dock or in flow, and this one has no business being in flow.
The summary line above it is the price of docking. A pinned bar covers rows, so the selection it acts on has to be restated: Kykar, Wind’s Fury · Jeskai. Desktop is untouched. There is no tab bar and no occlusion; the picker’s Selected sidebar keeps the button where it is. This is a phone-only promotion of a control that already exists, which is why it introduces no string but one.

Row 1 idle · row 2 pressed (--elevated plate, 6px) · row 3 removed, undo available.
The call: the ✕ was never a desktop idea
Swipe-to-remove is invisible and unreachable by keyboard; an edit mode is a mode for a one-tap action; a row overflow menu is two taps and a new pattern. The control already exists and is drawn hidden md:block. It gains a 44×44 hit box and loses the class.
This is also the locked parity constraint doing its job: the Add panel forbade width-only constructs, and a remove affordance that exists at one width is the same defect one surface over. The glyph stays 13px; the target is padding.
The call: undo lives where the row was
No toast. This app has no toast pattern, and the bottom of a phone is already spoken for by the rule above — a toast would either fight the dock or float over the tab bar. The removed row instead holds its slot as a spent slot, which is exactly what an accepted suggestion tile already does in Consider. Same principle, one design language.
No timer. #185 exists because the optimistic path makes the row vanish faster than a mis-click is noticed; a 5-second toast that also vanishes fixes half of it. The undo row persists until Undo is pressed, another card is removed (one undo row at a time, the older one settles), or the builder is left.
Only a last copy gets one. A stack of basics decrements and keeps its row, so the row itself is the feedback — decklist-row.tsx already branches on quantity <= 1 and the undo row hangs off that same branch.
Lifetime
It survives a tab switch. The undo row is decklist state, not row state, so going to Consider and back leaves it there — that is the likeliest interaction, and the one where a vanishing undo would hurt most. It survives a re-sort too. It clears on Undo, on the next removal, or on leaving the builder route. Nothing else, and never a timer.
The counts do not wait. The group count above a spent slot decrements immediately (Artifacts 6 → 5), along with the 99 count. A spent slot is a receipt, not a pending state — if the counts held for the life of the row, the deck would be lying about its own size.

The call: one control per cell, one disclosure per strip
The build drew the link beneath the cell because an anchor cannot nest in the button that owns the rationale. That is a real constraint, and the answer is to move the rationale rather than to add a second control: ten targets become six.
A short cell is a control; a met cell is a figure. The five identical consider → links were identical because they were not really about their cell — now the target and its label are the same object, and the accessible name carries the numbers off the cell face.
44px arrives for free. The desktop cell is 72px tall and the phone cell 62px; the caption no longer needs to be a target, so it can stay at 9px where it reads as a hint.
Rationale gets better, not worse. It was five separate expansions each pushing the strip taller; it is now one panel that answers the whole strip at once, which is how a player reads it anyway. The desktop worked-example callout is unchanged and sits below it.
Keyboard: cells are in DOM order, Enter switches to Consider and moves focus to the shelf heading — the same two-step the shell already performs, with focus stated.
#176 · unowned staples — a product call, then a tile
The premise was wrong, and it was ours
R1 decision 5 reads “suggestDeck() iterates ownedPool only, so every suggestion is already owned”. True of suggestDeck(), false of getDeckSuggestions(), which has appended up to five unowned EDHREC staples since #25. Three things inherited the error:
• the tile has no unowned variant, so those five say so only by the absence of a green dot that is aria-hidden;
• the provenance line says “N cards you already own” over a shelf that contains cards you do not;
• decision 5’s reasoning for keeping the owned filter out of Consider was that the axis has one value. It has two.
Product picks one. A — keep them, marked (drawn here, and Design’s recommendation): the deck already carries a buy list, #42 ships the import that fills it, and #132 is the backlog ticket for exactly this. B — drop the append at actions.ts:149-163: the premise becomes true, five staples leave the shelves, and none of the drawing below ships.
