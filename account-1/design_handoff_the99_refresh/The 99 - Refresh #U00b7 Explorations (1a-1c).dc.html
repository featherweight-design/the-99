UI refresh · three packages, one screen

The chrome gets quieter so the cards can talk.

Same screen — Decks, with filters open — drawn three ways. Each package answers all three open questions at once, because they interact: if colour is reserved for meaning, buttons cannot be coloured, and if the filter drawer is doing the work, the toolbar does not need chips. Compare them as wholes, then tell me which parts to keep.

Held constant across all three: the type system exactly as it stands (Cinzel headings, Plex Sans copy, Plex Mono for every number), the left icon rail you picked, and the four keepers — loading, Commander details, Availability, card details keep their layouts; only their controls inherit the new button and field styles.

Jump: 1a · Signal / 1b · Ambient / 1c · Ledger
1a Signal colour = status, never decoration

Near-monochrome shell. Every hue on screen is a fact: green complete, amber short, brass in use elsewhere, red unavailable. Because colour is spoken for, the primary button is ink on bone — the loudest thing in the room is never a mere action.

1c Ledger colour on the keyline, numbers in columns

The densest read: decks as a table with a 3px status keyline and aligned mono columns, so twenty decks compare without scrolling. Actions collapse into an icon cluster; the filter drawer opens with a typed token field for people who know what they want.

What I’d pick, and what mixes

1b’s cards with 1a’s buttons and 1c’s drawer is the strongest combination: art-led cards keep the app feeling like a collection, bone-fill pills keep actions legible for new players, and the persistent token drawer answers both the “hidden menus” and the “unintuitive navigation” complaints. The three axes are independent — name a colour, a button and a filter model and I’ll build that one out across Home, Commanders, Decks, the builder and the card modal.

Not yet drawn, and next once a direction lands: the error and timeout behaviour you called out on deckcheck — inline recoverable failures that never lose typed work, and a long-request pattern that keeps a partial result instead of timing out to nothing.
