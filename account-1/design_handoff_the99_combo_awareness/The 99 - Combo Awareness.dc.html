3a Turn 3 · the block closes by default — supersedes 2a’s density

A closed row that says what is inside, so the reader decides whether to spend the space.

Six lines is ~230px in the oracle column and it pushed the printing, the price and the pager below the fold on the card the reader actually opened. The fix is not a shorter list — it is a closed block, always, at every count. One 52px row, in the stack where it belongs, holding its position whether the reader opens it or never does.

And it is the honest answer to Hugh’s question — a list of fourteen stops being too much information when it is not on screen unless asked for.

closed — the default, and what most readers ever see
Two-card combos 1 in this deck · 1 you own · 4 you don’t ▾
The closed row is a count, and that is the one place a count is legal. Everywhere else a number would claim completeness — here it is a description of this list, the same licence +8 more had. It is a tally of what the block holds, not of what exists, and the * still carries the bound.

A state with a count of zero is not drawn. 4 you don’t alone, or 1 in this deck · 4 you don’t — never a 0 you own. Absence is not drawn, as everywhere in this round.

the rules that come with a closed block
Closed at every count, including one. A block that is sometimes a row and sometimes a list is a block whose position the reader cannot learn. One rule, one height, always — and at 49% of pieces having exactly one partner, that single line is also the cheapest thing to open.
The open state is remembered, per reader, not per card. A player who opens it on one card is telling us they care about combos; the next modal opens with the block already open. Closing it once turns it back off. One flag, and it is why the closed default costs the interested reader a single tap ever rather than one per card.
It is a disclosure, not an accordion. No sibling closes when this one opens — the other three blocks are not collapsible and this one does not become a set. It grows in place, downward, and the scroll position does not move.
Absent when nothing is detected, as before. No closed row saying 0, no reserved 52px. The closed row is itself the count, so a row is only ever drawn when there is something in it.
A11y: a real <button> with aria-expanded and aria-controls, its accessible name the label plus the counts as words — “two-card combos, one in this deck, one you own, four you don’t”. Focus stays on the button. The * is a separate target inside it and is not part of that name.
No motion. The block does not slide, fade or ease open. Round 1’s zero-net-motion rule holds — the chevron flips and the content is there.
Withdrawn from 2a: the six/four-line cap and both disclosure atoms inside this block. Kept from 2a: the placement in the stack, the scope line, the three states and their inks, the ordering rule, the pressable partner name, and the * with the bound and its Spellbook link.
this changes Design’s answer to Hugh’s question
Design now argues for keeping the unowned partners. The concern was fourteen combos blowing up a modal — and closed by default, they do not occupy a pixel until a reader asks for them. The closed row also puts the decision in the reader’s hands with the facts they need to make it: 1 in this deck · 1 you own · 4 you don’t tells them what is behind the row before they spend the space on it.
Still eng’s call, and still worth measuring — but the cut is now cheaper to make later than it was to make now: dropping the unowned lines is one filter, and the closed row’s counts change by themselves.

A pair fact sits between the card facts and the copy facts — and it is the one block that reads differently depending on where you opened from.

In the bracket rules is true of the card wherever you are standing. In your collection and In your decks are true of your copies, also wherever you are standing. A combo is neither: it is a fact about a pair, and half of it is somewhere that depends on the route. So the block goes second in the stack, and it names its own scope when it has one — otherwise the same card shows different words on two routes with nothing on screen explaining why.

open question for engineering — Hugh’s, carried verbatim, not Design’s to settle
“I have concerns about showing every two-card combo in a list — this could blow up a card details modal. Originally I thought we would just show what’s in a deck and what you own. We could technically link out to Spellbook for the ones you don’t own, but it is nice to have this utility in the app. I just wonder if showing 14 possible card combos is too much information. What’s your take?”
Design’s position, offered and not asserted: the cap already answers the height worry — six lines, four at 390, and 49% of pieces have exactly one partner — so the modal never opens taller than seven lines. The question the cap does not answer is whether the unowned partners belong at all.
If eng agrees with Hugh, the cut is clean and already drawn: keep in this deck and you own it, drop you don’t own it, and the block becomes a receipt about things you have rather than a catalogue. The ordering rule already puts the owned lines first, so the cut is literally a filter on the tail — no relayout, no new copy, and the marker’s sentence grows one clause (combos needing a card you don’t own are not listed). The disclosure and the six-line cap survive either way, because 21 of 249 owned pieces exceed ten partners before any ownership filter.
What Design would lose: the one case where an unowned partner earns its line is a cheap card — a $2 piece that closes a loop with something already in the deck is the best recommendation the app could make, and it is exactly what state 3 on the candidate surfaces exists to say. Dropping unowned partners from the modal does not remove that; it just means the modal stops repeating it. Design’s honest read: Hugh is probably right, and the version that ships should be measured on a real 14-partner card in a real modal before either of us is sure.

what this round refuses
A list. Hugh ruled it and the volume is the reason: 962 raw near-misses across 20 decks. A mark on a row a player is already reading costs nothing; a screen of 48 near-misses per deck costs attention nobody has.
A second ink, and a second glyph. Three states, one mark. The ownership difference is already drawn twice on every host that carries it.
Any count, badge or total. No 4 combos on a deck tile, no +1 after a partner name. Detection is bounded by design, so a number would be the first thing on the surface that is not true.
Sorting, filtering or promoting combo rows. The ranking is EDHREC synergy and stays that way. A mark says a fact; moving a row makes a recommendation, and nobody has argued for that one yet.
A tap target, a tooltip-only gloss, motion. Round 1’s rules carry over whole: the clause is inert, the row’s own tap opens the modal, and the glow is a static shadow.
A mark on the partner. The half already in the deck is the sentence’s object, not its subject. Marking both ends doubles the ink to say one thing.
