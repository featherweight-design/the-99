Open call 1 · where intent lives
Under the deck name, once it exists. Never as an empty field. Intent is a property of the deck, so it prints where the deck is named — but the ask lives inside the tuning panel, next to the payoff, because an empty field in the header taxes every deck for a feature one tab uses.
Open call 2 · six states, one container
Three fixed slots — heading, body, action row. Only the body changes. The heading never changes rank, the action row always holds exactly one primary in the same place, and nothing above or below the panel moves between states. Six states, one layout.
The wait, which is expected
The generating state is a manifest, not a spinner — the four things being read, each with its count, ticking off as they land. It is the only place in this app where a wait is expected, so it is the only place that gets to say what it is doing.
Locked · apply plus verdict

Locked · ownership is a label

Locked · manual only
Nothing generates on load, on save, or on the ninth game. A button, every time — so the wait is one the player chose, the cost is legible, and the panel is never a surface that spent money while you were reading it.

Why a note at all, and what it may not become
Eight axes cannot hold “I mulliganed to five”. The vocabulary is deliberately closed so it can be counted; the note is the pressure valve, and it is the only field on either surface that the player writes rather than picks.
The note is evidence, and it is quoted. When a rationale uses it, it appears in the player's own words in quotation marks with its date — your note from 26 Aug. Paraphrasing someone's sentence back to them as the app's reasoning is the fastest way this feature loses the room.
It never becomes a count. No note is summarised, clustered, or turned into a figure. #9's rule stands: every count on these surfaces comes from the marks, and the marks come from checkboxes the player ticked on purpose.
One note per game, and it edits by delete-and-relog — the same correction path §4 gives the rest of the row. Design flagged that cost in #9 and does not get to charge a second time for it.
Rejected: making the note required when a loss is logged. A form that demands an explanation for a bad night is a form people stop opening, and the fifteen-second promise is the reason the log has any data at all.
Rejected: prompts. No “what would you change?”, no suggested phrasings, no chips. A blank line with a cap gets a sentence; a prompt gets an answer to the prompt.

The call, in one paragraph
Intent belongs to the deck, so it prints where the deck is named. It is not a tuning setting — it is the answer to “what is this pile trying to do”, it is true on the Decklist and on Consider, and burying it in one tab would make it a preference for a feature rather than a fact about a deck.
But the empty state does not belong there. An editable line in the header costs every deck a dashed describe this deck row, forever, to serve one panel — and a header full of grey invitations is how a product tells you it has run out of things to say. So absent means absent.
The ask lives where it pays. Inside the tuning panel, immediately above the button, phrased as a sharpening rather than a requirement, and it names the actual gap it would close. Two entry points, one field, one string.
Rejected: intent as a prerequisite. Generation runs without it. The panel is allowed to say the suggestions would be better with it — once, in the ask, not as a banner over the results.
Rejected: a picker — casual / focused / cEDH, or an archetype select. Every one of those is a label the player has to translate into their own table, and the model gets more from eleven honest words than from a bucket. It is also the exact place a product starts telling people what kind of player they are.
Rejected: seeding intent from the decklist. The app can describe a pile; it cannot know what you wanted. A guess printed under the deck name as if the player wrote it is a lie with an edit button.
The seeded case is different, and labelled. When The 99 built the deck it does know the brief it built to, so it prints it — with the provenance disc, a from the build chip, and the same edit affordance. Attribution is the whole difference between a seed and a guess.
Cap 120, one line, no markdown. Long enough for a sentence, short enough that it never becomes a second heading, and it is quoted to the model verbatim like the note.
Surface 4 · the tuning panel — one container, three slots, six states
The container, drawn once — every state below fills these three slots and moves nothing
slot 1 · heading
Cinzel 19px, always. One line, always the same rank, never a state colour. It says what the panel is for in four of six states and what happened in the other two.
slot 2 · body
The only slot that changes shape. A sentence, a manifest, three cards, a reason, or a failure — all of them a single column at the panel's full width, so the container's own geometry is state-independent.
slot 3 · action row
Exactly one primary, always in the same corner, plus a mono note beside it. Its label changes; its position and rank never do. Disabled in one state, in flight in one, absent in none.
Six states, not six layouts
The panel is one box in one place, and it never leaves. It sits below the aggregate and above the history, at --surface-sunken on 1px --border, radius 12px, and it renders in all six states including the ones with nothing to say. A panel that appears and disappears is a panel that moves the history under the player's cursor.
The heading never carries state. No colour, no icon, no count. Six headings, one voice, and a screen reader hears the same landmark every time.
The action row is a fixed 56px band. One primary, left, same corner in every state — so the button never moves under a tap that arrives a beat after a state change, which is the exact failure a five-second generation makes likely.
Only the body's height changes, and it grows downward. Nothing above the panel reflows and the history is pushed, never covered.
Colour lives in the body only, and only in two states: --warning on the cited Fix axis, --danger on a failure's one-line reason. Results are brass and ink.

Manual only — what that buys, and what it costs
Nothing generates on load, on save, or on the third game. Locked upstream, and the panel is drawn to make it feel deliberate rather than lazy: state 2 is not an empty state, it is a briefing. It says what will be read, what will come back, and how long it takes.
No badge, no bell, no “3 new suggestions”. There is nothing to notify about — suggestions exist only after a player asked for them, in the tab they asked from.
Cost, stated: a player who logs nine games and never presses the button gets nothing from this feature. That is the price of not spending their money or their attention without being asked, and state 2's body is the mitigation — it is written to be worth pressing.
Re-running is the same button, relabelled Suggest again, in the same corner, after results. Results are not cleared until new ones land — nothing unmounts mid-write, and a re-run that fails leaves the old suggestions exactly where they were.
One live-region message per settle: Reading your deck at the start, then Three suggestions ready, or the reason, or the failure. Never one per manifest line.

The card, decision by decision
The pair is one line, and the arrow is the whole idea. Cut, arrow, add — one 31px row with a 22×31 thumbnail each side. A suggestion that only named the add would be a shopping list; the cut is the part that costs something, so it keeps equal weight and equal size.
Four rows, 9px padding, and nothing labelled twice. The card is deliberately tight — three of them plus a footer have to fit one screen, and this product's boxes run roomy. What was cut to get there: the Cut / Add column headings (the strike-through and the arrow already say direction), the cut card's type line (it is in your deck; you know what it is), the rule above the rationale, and 5px of every gap. The rationale drops to 12.5px at 1.5 and the chips to 9.5px. Nothing legible was traded: 12.5px body ink and 9.5px mono on --surface are the product's existing floors, and the tap targets did not move.
The cut is struck through in --fg-body, not in --danger. It is not a mistake and it was not an error — it is a card that is not pulling its weight in nine specific games.
Ownership is a label, per the lock. ×1 owned in --success, not owned in --fg-faint with the price beside it. It never filters, never ranks, never sorts, and never suppresses a card. The right card is the right card.
Price only when unowned, and only as one mono figure. A price on a card you already have is noise, and a total across the three swaps would turn a tuning panel into a basket. That is what the shopping list is for, and applying an unowned add puts it there.
The rationale is one sentence and it must contain a count with its denominator. That is a hard rule on the model's output, not a hope: no percentages, no nets, no often, no consistently. If it cannot cite a number it counted, it has nothing to say.
Evidence is cited as chips, and each names its source. games, note, decklist, intent — the same four things the manifest read, so the player can trace any claim back to something they typed. A card with no citable evidence is not shown.
The note is quoted, never paraphrased, in quotation marks with its date.
Three, not ten. Ten swaps is a rebuild, and a rebuild is not a suggestion. Three fits on one screen at 900px, and a player can hold three trade-offs in their head at once.
Both actions are non-destructive-looking and one is real. Apply this swap is brass outline, not brass solid — Build this deck owns solid brass and applying a swap is not the app's goal.
The consequence prints before the tap. The mono line beside the buttons is the verdict this swap would produce — the deck's count and status, plus the shopping list if the add is unowned. Apply-plus-verdict, and the verdict half is legible in advance.

One row, same position in the list, no gap and no animation out. A dismissed suggestion that vanishes moves the two below it under the cursor — and a player who taps Not this one by accident has no way back.
in placeNeither action removes the card. Accepted keeps the pair, gains a --success frame and an applied stamp; dismissed collapses to a one-line row in the same slot. The list never reorders and never shortens under you.
the verdictThe applied stamp is followed by the deck's verdict, in the shipped status wording — 99 / 99 · complete or 99 / 99 · 1 to find — then the shopping consequence in one clause. undoPut it back is a real reversal of a real mutation, not a five-second window — it is available for as long as the suggestion is on screen, and it restores both cards and the shopping list. This is the undo #9 wanted and could not have, because here the thing being undone is still visible.
no ratingDismissing asks no questions. No “why not?”, no thumbs, no reason picker. A feedback prompt on a rejected suggestion charges the player for the app's mistake, and there is nothing in this release that would read the answer.
persistenceApplied and dismissed states survive a reload and last until the next run replaces them. The footer counts what is left: 1 of 3 applied · 1 dismissed — counts with denominators, like everything else.
refusalIf the apply mutation is refused, the card returns to its open state with the shipped MutationError under its own action row — not at the panel level. The failure belongs to the swap that failed.
The counting rule, applied to a model — what the audit can check
1. No string on any of the five surfaces contains % against a count. Including model output. A percentage in a rationale is a defect, not a wording preference.
2. No rendered string contains a signed integer or the word net. net still sorts and still never shows its face.
3. Every count in a rationale or a citation chip carries its denominator in the same element — 7 of 9, 11 of 99, 3 of 4 read, 78 / 140.
4. Every suggestion carries at least one citation chip, and every chip names one of the four read sources.
5. A quoted note appears in quotation marks with its date and is byte-identical to what the player typed.
6. No card is shown as an add without an ownership label; price appears if and only if the label is not owned.
7. Ownership appears in no filter, no sort, no ranking input, and no empty-state escape hatch on this surface.
8. The panel renders in all six states; the action row's primary is in the same position in all six.
9. PATTERN_MIN is imported from #9's copy block. The number 3 is inlined nowhere in #10.
The enforcement point matters. Rules 1–3 are checked after generation and before render: a rationale that fails them is not shown — it counts as a rejected candidate, and if all of them fail, the panel lands in failure class four. Design is not asking for a prompt that politely requests no percentages; it is asking for a filter that drops the suggestion.

Failure, and the difference between five and six
State 5 is not a failure and is not drawn as one. The run worked, and the answer is that the deck has not moved. So it gets the panel's ordinary ink, a reason in three countable lines, and a primary that lets the player insist. --danger appears nowhere in it.
The reason is the whole state. “No new suggestions” with nothing after it is the app shrugging. Three lines — games since the last run, the strongest axis, whether intent changed — and the player can see for themselves that there is nothing to say, and exactly what would change that.
Four classes, one shape. Each is the same three slots with a --danger tag beside the heading, one plain sentence, a line confirming what was preserved, and one primary. No error codes, no stack, no something went wrong.
Every failure says what survived. Your games, your note, your list and any suggestions already on screen. That line is the point of the state: the fear a failed AI call raises is that it did something, and the copy answers it before it is asked.
The tag is the only place --danger is allowed on these surfaces. Not the heading, not the border, not the button. A red panel for a service that will work in a minute is a fire alarm for a delay.
Class 4 is the interesting one. Generation succeeded and produced nothing usable — every candidate broke a rule (a card outside the colour identity, a cut that is not in the deck, a rationale with no countable evidence, a percentage). The player is told it produced nothing it could stand behind, which is true and is better than showing them one.
Retry is a button, never automatic. Manual-only applies to the second attempt as much as the first, and a silent retry loop is how a wait becomes a bill.
Open: these four labels are Design's inference — §6 was not readable from this project. The count is four and the shape is one; if §6's classes are named or split differently, it is a copy edit in the copy block and no redraw.

What Design is asking eng for
Two nullable text columns and one cap each — deck_games.note at 140, decks.intent at 120. Both single-line, both quoted verbatim to the model, neither ever parsed for structure.
A post-generation validator that drops candidates, not a prompt that asks nicely. The nine mechanical checks are in the panel to the left, and rules 1–3 are the ones that must run server-side before anything renders.
