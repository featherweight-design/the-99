The heaviest scrim in the band sits exactly where the face is

You read it as two overlays and the effect is the one you describe, but the shipped mechanism is worse than two scrims and simpler: a cover band is a single gradient that holds 78% over the top 31% of the band, thins across the middle, and settles at 14% all the way to the foot — COVER_SCRIM_STOPS.top. R1 fixed the edge — one bottom block, no top scrim — but sized the new ramp as a fraction of the band, so a taller band scaled its own darkness up with it and gave the height back. That is the gap you are seeing, and it is one number's worth of change.

hold  73% over the bottom labelAt px
ramp  over 0.48 × labelAt px above it
clear above that — no scrim at all
Read that as a change of UNIT, because it is the whole fix. labelAt ships today as a percentage — globals.css names 55% on the commander hero and 57% on the deck spine in its reduced-transparency comment. After this round it is pixels from the foot. A percentage is exactly what made a taller band scale its own darkness; pixels are what make added height land on art. Every settled value — 40, 46, 57, 66, 78 — is px, and the 57 here is detail mobile, not the deck spine's shipped 57%: same digits, different unit, different surface. Rename the prop if that trap worries you.
And labelAt is the block's MEASURED height, not its intended one. I costed the mobile deck header at 57px — one title line plus a meta row — and at 390px that 20px Cinzel title wraps to three lines in its ~302px column, so the real block is 83px. Derived from 57, the 73% hold lands where the block ends instead of where it starts, and the title's first line sits on unscrimmed art: precisely the failure this round exists to remove, reintroduced by an optimistic number. Every band here is now derived from the wrapped height, which is why deck details mobile unveils 57% rather than the 71% I first claimed.
And the consumer's signature has to change with it, or the round ships the bug inverted. bottomScrimStops(labelAt) emits [73, labelAt], and that second element is a gradient position percentage — its own comment says "the block's top edge as a percentage of band height". Hand it a px value and bottomScrimStops(46) holds at 46%, 114px down a 248 band, where every drawing here puts it at 81%. That is the scrim landing almost exactly where this round is trying to remove it.
bottomScrimStops(labelAtPx, band) =
  [[0, 0],
   [0, (band − 1.48·at) / band × 100],
   [73, (band − at) / band × 100],
   [73, 100]]
A px labelAt cannot produce a percentage position without the band height, so the band becomes a second argument. The shipped 0.48 and 73 are unchanged — 1.48 is just at + 0.48·at folded together. Emitting px stops instead of % and keeping one argument works equally well; pick one, but do not leave the one-arg percentage signature next to a px value.

Every render of a commander's image, drawn once under one treatment. The brief locks seven decisions and leaves five calls; this document makes the five, enumerates every element left standing at a top edge, and states the amendments to design_handoff_the99_art_wash/ and design_handoff_the99_deck_details_wash/ one by one, so the audit can check them individually rather than re-deriving the wash.

§3.1 · the height, and the smallest set of sizes — decision 4
One number cannot serve fifteen, and the arithmetic says why
Commander detail's mobile band is 1.6/1, so at 390px it is 244px tall today. The deck tile's column runs 189px wide at 640 and 445px at 1440. A single fixed height that is taller than 244 on a 189px-wide tile is a portrait box holding a landscape crop, and decision 3 requires taller everywhere. R1 argued that down to a set of two; your review took it to four — the rows priced by scroll at 208, and the desktop header at 288 to carry the wide portrait you picked.
art_crop is 626×457 · 1.37 : 1
168 × 1.37 = 230px portrait
208 × 1.37 = 285px portrait
248 × 1.37 = 340px portrait
288 → portrait 560, NOT 395
The desktop band inverts the rule, and it is the only one that does. Everywhere else the height is chosen and the portrait follows from it. On deck details desktop you picked the portrait's shape first, so 560 is the chosen number and the height follows it — 288 is what keeps the crop from scaling past ~42%. Deriving 288 × 1.37 = 395 here loses the whole of 3a §E, including the ≥1200px floor that comes with it.
What governs which: not viewport, not route — what the box is for
A render is one of six things, and every question is answerable without looking at a screen size. Is the image the point of this box — a page header, or a grid cell whose job is telling you which deck is which? → header band 288. Is it a grid cell telling you which of several things this is? → cell band 248, and never in a column under ~330px. Is it a full-width row you scan down? → row band 208, the one class priced by scroll. Is it a secondary cell on a page about something else? → resume band 168. Is it identifying a row you are choosing among? → thumb. Is the player meant to read the printed card? → card face.
R1 concluded this dissolved the spine's breakpoint fork. It nearly does. Renders 09 and 10 are the same class differing only in the crop their own box's aspect earns — but they no longer share a height, and 3a §E found one breakpoint survives at ~1200px, governing the desktop portrait's width. Opaque takes the control out of the contrast problem entirely. Ink on var(--bg) is the page's own body contrast, which each theme already guarantees — so no plate on any of the fifteen needs a per-theme measurement, and none needs a scrim underneath. That is the whole argument for convergence, and it is the argument decision 5 needs in order to be safe.
The blur retires with the translucency: it has nothing to blur through an opaque fill, so it was a compositing cost with no visual effect. The deck overflow's desktop treatment is this plate already — so the convergence is one control adopting the other, not a new drawing.
Amended in 4a: nothing worded ships, so the 999 arm retires with it. Every plate is now a 26–32px drawn box centred in a 44px target, carrying an icon and no label — the plate itself is what makes the control visible against art, which is the job the words were doing. That makes aria-label required rather than nice, because dropping the text removes the only accessible name these controls had. It is the one line of this spec that is not visual.
Where the plate stops, and the ramp starts
The boundary has to be stated or the rule over-applies. The ramp protects the label block; plates protect everything above it. So the rename pencil on the deck's mobile band takes no plate — it sits inside the block beside the 20px title, on ground held at 73%, and keeps COVER_INK_SHADOW like every string around it. A plate there would be the only chrome in a block whose whole treatment is ink on a held ramp.
Conversely a 44px phone target above the block is bare today and cannot stay bare: the top scrim was the thing protecting it.
